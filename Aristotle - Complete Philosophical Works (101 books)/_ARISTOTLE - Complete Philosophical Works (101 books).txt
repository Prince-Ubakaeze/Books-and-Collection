* Aristotle - Complete Philosophical Works (101 books)

ARISTOTLE (384–322 BCE) was an ancient Greek philosopher and polymath, and one of the most influential thinkers in Western intellectual history.  A student of Plato and later tutor to Alexander the Great, Aristotle developed an intellectual project that sought to explain nature, knowledge, ethics, and political life without appealing to transcendent Forms.  Instead, he grounded philosophy in careful observation, logical analysis, and an attention to the purposes (telē) inherent in natural and human activities.  His work marks a decisive turn toward an empirical and classificatory approach that would shape philosophy and science for centuries.

Unlike many earlier thinkers, Aristotle wrote not dialogues but treatises, most of which are thought to be lecture notes compiled by later editors.  These texts range across logic, metaphysics, natural science, psychology, ethics, rhetoric, and political theory, forming a remarkably integrated body of work.  Central to this system is Aristotle’s development of formal logic, preserved in the collection later known as the "Organon", which includes the CATEGORIES, ON INTERPRETATION, and the PRIOR and POSTERIOR ANALYTICS.  Here Aristotle analyzes the structure of propositions, syllogistic reasoning, and scientific demonstration, laying the foundations for logic as a discipline.

Aristotle’s metaphysical and natural philosophical writings aim to explain being and change in the natural world. In the PHYSICS, he investigates motion, causation, and nature as an internal principle of change, while the METAPHYSICS addresses more fundamental questions about being qua being, substance (ousia), and the relation between form and matter.  The doctrine of the four causes—material, formal, efficient, and final—provides a unifying explanatory framework across these works, culminating in the concept of the unmoved mover as the ultimate explanatory principle of cosmic motion.

Equally influential are Aristotle’s practical and humanistic writings, especially the NICOMACHEAN ETHICS and the POLITICS.  In the former, Aristotle articulates a virtue-based account of moral life centered on eudaimonia (flourishing) and the cultivation of character through habituation and practical wisdom (phronēsis).  The Politics extends this inquiry to the level of the polis, arguing that political institutions exist to enable human flourishing and offering a comparative analysis of constitutions.  Together with works such as RHETORIC and POETICS, which examine persuasion and artistic representation, Aristotle’s corpus presents a comprehensive vision of human reason operating across theoretical, practical, and productive domains.
